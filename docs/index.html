ukraine building damage assessment

using free satellite radar data to check which buildings were damaged in the war, and how well that actually works

the problem

the standard way to build a "building damage detection" project is xview2: train a model on xbd, a dataset of before/after satellite photos with hand drawn building outlines and damage labels. the problem is xbd only has natural disasters (hurricanes, earthquakes, wildfires), zero war damage. and there's no free high resolution satellite imagery for ukraine to build an xbd style dataset for the war either. maxar's free imagery program was checked directly, it covers natural disasters worldwide, not this war.

what real researchers and journalists (including the economist) actually use for ukraine is a different approach. instead of teaching a model to spot buildings in a photo, start from building locations you already know, and check how the radar signal at each one changed over time. this only needs sentinel-1 satellite data, which is free and public, and radar works through clouds and at night.

the three ingredients

any damage model needs 3 things: where the buildings are, what the satellite saw there over time, and for at least some buildings, real ground truth on what actually happened. each comes from a different source:

  • building locations, microsoft building footprints, a free global dataset of building outlines. no idea about damage, just shapes.
  • satellite signal, sentinel-1 radar, free via google earth engine.
  • ground truth, unosat (un satellite centre) manually looked at real high resolution photos and marked every damaged building they could see, for specific ukrainian cities. real human judgement, done once, not automated.

pwtt: the published project this builds on

pwtt (pixel-wise t-test) is a peer reviewed, open source project that already combined all 3 sources above for 19 ukrainian cities: unosat's damage points joined onto microsoft's building footprints, plus a simple statistical change detection score computed per building from sentinel-1. it's the same method the economist used to track ukraine damage nationwide. pwtt's detection rule has no training involved, it's a fixed formula: flag a building damaged if its radar change score crosses a threshold.

what we built on top

we pulled the raw sentinel-1 signal ourselves (not just pwtt's precomputed summary numbers), engineered our own features from it, and trained actual classifiers (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, plain scikit-learn, no neural network, buildings are roughly the size of one satellite pixel here, so there's no real image to feed a computer vision model) to see whether a trained model can do better than pwtt's fixed rule.

evaluated properly: leave one city out cross validation across all 19 cities, each one held out and tested on a model that never saw it during training.

the honest result

across all 19 cities, pwtt's simple baseline wins on average (mean AUC 73.8 vs our best model, gradient boosting, at 72.4). our model only wins outright on 7 of 19 cities.

this matches pwtt's own published finding that a simple statistical method rivals more complex ones here. this project independently confirmed that, rather than assuming it.

we also checked whether a model helps more in some cities than others. it does, but the pattern is more about sample size than damage severity. in cities with very few actually damaged buildings (under roughly 90), results swing wildly in both directions, some of the biggest wins and the biggest losses in the whole dataset are both in this group, which is the signature of noise, not a real effect. we tested this directly with a permutation test (randomly reshuffling which city gets which result, thousands of times). a threshold based "use the model above X% damage" rule that looked promising did no better than 26.6% of purely random shuffles, not nearly convincing enough to trust. the more honest, weaker claim that does hold up: at higher damage rates it's calmer and leans slightly toward the model (4 wins, 3 losses among cities over 5% damage), suggestive, not proven.

a current snapshot: two real bugs found and fixed, one open question left honestly unresolved

this section applies the trained model to recent satellite data (2026). it's kept separate from the validated result above on purpose, this part doesn't fully work, and the investigation into why is more useful shown honestly than hidden.

testing it against two cities with almost zero real historical damage (kremenchuk, sumy, both 0.1% in 2022) exposed a genuine, confirmed bug: a feature measuring how much a building's signal wobbles across satellite passes was built from a different number of passes than training ever saw (coverage is denser now than in 2022, likely a new satellite having joined the fleet). matching each city's exact training pass count fixed this for both the narrow window and a second, similarly shaped "wide" window feature.

the fix fully resolves kremenchuk (45.2% to 0.8% flagged, matching its true 0.1% almost exactly), clean proof the bug was real. it only partly resolves sumy (48.0% to 29.5%, still far above its true 0.1%).

also found and fixed a separate, unrelated bug: an early mariupol pull used a different satellite orbit than its 2022 baseline, corrupting 95% of its readings. refetched with the orbit forced to match.

"control city" turned out to be the wrong assumption. looked up kremenchuk and sumy's actual history since 2022. sumy suffered a deadly missile strike in november 2024, the deadliest attack on ukrainian civilians since 2023 in april 2025 (35 killed, 20 buildings damaged), and more strikes in june 2025. kremenchuk's oil refinery was hit by 69 missiles and 260+ drones over the course of the war, plus residential strikes in 2024 to 2026. these were never quiet, untouched cities, some of the elevated reading could be real, accumulated damage, not pure error.

the most direct test available: pull multiple years and look for a jump at the known strike dates. did exactly that, summer snapshots for kremenchuk (2023 to 2026) and windows bracketing sumy's two worst strikes, tracking the share of buildings with an elevated raw signal (not run through the model, just the raw numbers):

kremenchuk is flat across all 4 years, no trend, consistent with its resolved near zero prediction. sumy shows a real, gradual rise (5.2% to 8.6%) but not a sharp jump at either strike date specifically, most likely because the april 2025 strike damaged about 20 buildings out of sumy's 28,265, far too small an event to move a city wide average. the gradual rise points to something diffuse accumulating over years rather than one clean cause.

bottom line, and where we deliberately stopped: sumy is genuinely changing over time in a way kremenchuk isn't, which is real evidence this isn't pure noise, but it doesn't fully explain the size of the reading, and we stopped chasing it further here on purpose. an honestly unresolved question is an acceptable way to end a clearly scoped side investigation, it would not be an acceptable way to end the validated result above, which is why these are kept separate. one more caveat regardless: the satellite photo underneath the buildings is Esri's basemap, and we don't know its exact capture date.
